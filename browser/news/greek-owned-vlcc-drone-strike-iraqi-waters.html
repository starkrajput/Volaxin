Greek-Owned VLCC Drone Strike in Iraqi Waters Tests Risk Calculus

An attack on the 302,500 dwt New Andros off Al-Faw exposes the vulnerability of crude exports in the northern Gulf and forces charterers to re-evaluate regional security assumptions.

Published 9 Sept 2026 4 min read By Aditya Baghdad

A Greek-owned VLCC carrying two million barrels of fuel oil was struck by a drone off Al-Faw. The incident forces a sharp reassessment of risk in northern Gulf waters.

Key takeaways
  • The 302,500 dwt Greek-owned VLCC New Andros was struck by a drone at approximately 06:00 local time east of Al-Faw.
  • The vessel was carrying around two million barrels of Iraqi fuel oil when the attack occurred in Iraqi territorial waters.
  • Iraqi rescue vessels successfully extinguished the resulting fire onboard, and all 22 crewmembers were confirmed safe.
  • The incident challenges prevailing assumptions regarding the relative safety of northern Gulf oil loading terminals against asymmetric attacks.
In short

The Greek-owned VLCC New Andros, carrying two million barrels of fuel oil, was struck by a drone in Iraqi territorial waters east of Al-Faw. While local rescue vessels extinguished the fire and all 22 crewmembers remained safe, the attack exposes critical vulnerabilities in northern Gulf export routes.

How Does the New Andros Attack Shift Northern Gulf Risk Assessments?

The drone strike on the 302,500 dwt very large crude carrier New Andros inside Iraqi territorial waters demonstrates that upper Gulf loading zones, long viewed as shielded compared to the Bab el-Mandeb strait or the Red Sea, remain directly exposed to asymmetric aerial threats. When the laden tanker was hit east of Al-Faw at approximately 06:00 local time, carrying roughly two million barrels of Iraqi fuel oil, it shattered the baseline assumption that Iraqi offshore terminals offered a safer commercial haven than southern Red Sea lanes. According to Splash247, the resulting fire was extinguished by local rescue vessels and all 22 crewmembers remained safe, yet the operational fallout for fleet managers, charterers, and hull underwriters is immediate and severe. Marine superintendents managing tonnage out of Basra and Khor al-Amaya must now factor active drone interception protocols into standard voyage planning, regardless of whether a vessel is transiting international choke points or operating inside sovereign anchorage zones.

This incident forces shipowners to discard regional complacency. The northern Gulf has historically functioned as a reliable artery for heavy crude and fuel oil exports, even when geopolitical tensions spiked further south. Now, technical teams are scrambling to review shipboard hardening measures, such as citadel readiness and early warning optical surveillance, for vessels loading at terminals like Basra Oil Terminal. The physical reality of a two-million-barrel cargo burning within sight of the coastline changes the insurance calculus overnight. P&I clubs and hull underwriters will undoubtedly look closely at how war risk premiums apply to northern Gulf liftings, especially if attribution proves difficult and additional asymmetric strikes follow.

What Operational Vulnerabilities Did the Al-Faw Incident Expose?

Operating a mega-scale tanker in restricted coastal waters leaves little margin for error when kinetic threats emerge from unannounced vectors. The New Andros was struck in relatively confined waters east of Al-Faw, a zone marked by heavy vessel density, pilotage handovers, and complex navigational traffic schemes designed to manage outbound VLCC drafts. When an aerial drone penetrates this environment, bridge teams have seconds rather than minutes to execute defensive maneuvering, assuming the threat is even detected by standard commercial radar suites. Most commercial tankers are built for operational efficiency and environmental containment, not electronic warfare defense or kinetic shock absorption. The primary failure mode in these scenarios is not immediate hull catastrophic failure, but rather the ignition of volatile upper-deck atmospheres or cargo manifold areas during loading or bunkering operations.

Fleet managers must evaluate whether their current crew security training covers low-altitude aerial incursions while stationary or at slow speeds during STS operations or single-point mooring approaches. The speed of response from Iraqi rescue vessels prevented a localized fire from turning into a total loss, but reliance on local port state emergency response cannot substitute for onboard damage control readiness. Technical superintendents should immediately audit fixed firefighting foam systems on deck, remote emergency shutdown valves, and personal protective equipment staging areas. Crew fatigue also spikes dramatically when operating in designated high-risk areas, as lookouts are forced into continuous high-alert states that degrade standard navigational watchkeeping efficacy over multi-day loading windows.

The Gulf Tanker Risk Matrix

Navigating the complex threat environment of the Middle East requires a systematic approach to vessel deployment, security posture, and contractual protection. To assist fleet managers and charterers in evaluating exposure, we have established the Gulf Tanker Risk Matrix, a structured decision-making framework designed to grade voyage risk based on geographic proximity to coastal friction points and cargo volatility.

  • Tier 1: Low-Density Open Waters — Open international traffic separation schemes well clear of littoral conflict zones, requiring standard BMP5 security protocols and routine watchkeeping without specialized hardening.
  • Tier 2: Controlled Loading Terminals — Sovereign anchorage and terminal zones like Al-Faw where local naval assets provide primary security, but where low-altitude asymmetric threats can bypass perimeter defenses.
  • Tier 3: High-Threat Choke Points — Confined corridors subject to direct kinetic interdiction, requiring active electronic monitoring, hardened citadels, and pre-agreed charter party safe-port revocations.

The physical reality of a two-million-barrel cargo burning within sight of the coastline changes the insurance calculus overnight. Fleet managers can no longer treat northern Gulf liftings as low-risk backwaters exempt from asymmetric maritime warfare.

What to Watch Next

Three critical operational signals will dictate how commercial shipping responds to the evolving threat profile in Iraqi waters over the coming weeks:

  • War Risk Premium Adjustments: Monitor hull and machinery underwriters for sudden endorsements, additional premiums, or geographical limit revisions covering Iraqi territorial waters east of Al-Faw.
  • Terminal Security Protocols: Track any mandatory security briefings or procedural updates issued by the Iraqi Ports Company or the Basra Oil Company regarding anchorage management and approach routes.
  • Charter Party Clause Invocations: Watch for an increase in war risk or unsafe port dispute notices filed by owners chartering VLCCs for Persian Gulf and northern Gulf liftings.
