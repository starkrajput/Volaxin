Hunter Group VLCC Charter Dispute Escalates Past $55M

Unpaid hire claims mount as Oslo-listed owner faces a sixth consecutive month of reduced charter payments.

Published 8 Sept 2026 3 min read By Aditya Oslo

Hunter Group's long-running VLCC charter dispute has escalated beyond $55m following the largest monthly shortfall to date from its long-term counterparty.

Key takeaways
  • Hunter Group's total unpaid charter hire claim has surpassed $55m following mounting monthly shortfalls.
  • The unnamed long-term counterparty paid approximately $11.75m less than required for the August billing cycle.
  • The August deficit represents the sixth consecutive month of reduced payments under the charter agreements.
  • Hunter Group is an Oslo-listed company operating within the competitive very large crude carrier sector.
In short

Hunter Group's VLCC charter dispute surpassed $55m after its long-term counterparty underpaid by roughly $11.75m for August, marking the sixth consecutive month of reduced hire payments received by the Oslo-listed tanker owner.

A high-stakes commercial fallout in the tanker sector is deepening as unpaid charter hire mounts across major fleet assets. Fleet managers and commercial operators are closely watching the escalating financial exposure facing Oslo-listed tanker owner Hunter Group as its long-term charter dispute intensifies. According to Splash247, the total outstanding claim has now climbed past $55m following the largest monthly payment shortfall recorded since the dispute began.

How Did the VLCC Charter Dispute Escalate?

The total unpaid hire claim for Hunter Group surpassed $55m in September 2026 after the company's unnamed long-term counterparty failed to meet its full contractual obligations for August. During that month, the counterparty paid approximately $11.75m less than what Hunter Group maintains was legally due under the governing charter party agreements. This massive monthly deficit marks a troubling sixth consecutive month of reduced payments, exposing the severe friction that can occur when long-term fixtures collide with sudden market volatility or shifting charterer balance sheets. For shipowners and commercial operators managing large crude carriers, the situation highlights the persistent credit risks inherent in long-term bareboat or time-charter arrangements.

Commercial relationships at this scale rarely fracture overnight, and the sequence of underpayments points to a sustained deterioration in the counterparty's compliance with agreed hire rates. When a charterer begins remitting partial payments month after month, shipowners face immediate cash flow pressures alongside complex legal calculations regarding withdrawal rights and lien enforcement.

What Are the Commercial Risks for Owners?

Extended charter party disputes over unpaid hire threaten liquidity and complicate fleet valuation across the broader tanker segment. When a counterparty withholds millions in monthly hire, the vessel-owning entity must absorb the shortfall or initiate aggressive legal recovery measures that can disrupt commercial operations. Key factors currently impacting the dispute include:

  • Six consecutive months of reduced hire remittances from the unnamed long-term counterparty.
  • A single-month deficit of approximately $11.75m recorded for the August billing cycle.
  • Total outstanding claims ballooning past the $55m threshold, straining corporate liquidity.
  • Uncertainty regarding the eventual enforcement of maritime liens or formal arbitration proceedings.

Navigating such heavy financial discrepancies requires rigorous oversight from legal counsel and maritime P&I correspondents. Charterers and shipowners alike are evaluating how this case might influence future fixture negotiations, particularly regarding performance guarantees and default clauses in high-value crude oil carrier contracts.

What to watch next

Industry observers should monitor three critical developments as this high-value dispute progresses. First, watch for any formal arbitration filings or legal notices served in maritime jurisdictions regarding the breached charter parties. Second, track whether Hunter Group moves to withdraw the affected very large crude carriers from the existing commercial employment. Third, observe if the unnamed counterparty issues a public response or restructuring proposal to address the mounting $55m liability.
