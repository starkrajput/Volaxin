Oil Record Book Entries: Codes, Examples & the Mistakes That Cause Detentions

Incorrect Oil Record Book entries are among the most frequently recorded deficiencies in Port State Control inspections worldwide — and unlike most findings, an ORB error can escalate from a deficiency to a detention, a coastal-State investigation, or a criminal MARPOL prosecution. The book itself is simple. The discipline it demands is not. This guide covers what each code actually means, how a correct entry reads, and precisely which other records an inspector will hold it against.

What the Oil Record Book Is — and Who Must Keep It

The Oil Record Book (ORB) is the statutory log of oil-related operations required under MARPOL Annex I. It exists to make oil handling on board traceable and auditable — every litre of sludge, bilge water and bunker fuel accounted for from generation to disposal.

  • Part I — Machinery space operations. Required on every ship of 400 GT and above (and oil tankers of 150 GT and above).
  • Part II — Cargo/ballast operations. Required additionally on oil tankers of 150 GT and above.

Three procedural rules underpin everything else: entries are made operation by operation (not batched at month end), each entry is signed by the officer in charge of that operation, and each completed page is signed by the Master. The book must be readily available for inspection and retained for the period required by the regulation.

⚠️

The ORB is a legal document. In several jurisdictions, false entries have been prosecuted as criminal offences independent of any actual pollution — the record itself is the offence. That is why "tidy it up later" is never an acceptable practice.

The Codes Explained: Part I, A to I

Most ORB deficiencies begin with a code letter chosen incorrectly. Part I operations are coded as follows:

CodeOperationTypical entries
ABallasting or cleaning of oil fuel tanksBallasting a fuel tank; cleaning a tank previously containing oil fuel
BDischarge of dirty ballast or cleaning water from oil fuel tanksDischarge of the water from a tank covered under Code A
CCollection, internal transfer and disposal of oil residues (sludge)Weekly collection of residues; transfer between sludge tanks; landing sludge ashore; incineration
DNon-automatic discharge overboard, transfer or disposal of bilge water accumulated in machinery spacesManual operation of the 15 ppm equipment overboard; pumping bilges to a holding tank; landing bilge water ashore
EAutomatic discharge overboard, transfer or disposal of bilge waterWhere the system operates automatically — record the system's operation
FCondition of the oil filtering (15 ppm) equipmentFailures, repairs, alarm tests and maintenance of the OWS and 15 ppm alarm
GAccidental or other exceptional discharges of oilSpills, leaks, overflows — circumstances, quantity, reasons
HBunkering of fuel or bulk lubricating oilEach bunkering: place, time, type, quantity, tanks
IAdditional operational procedures and general remarksAnything not covered above; explanations and clarifications
🎯

The single most cited confusion: C versus D. Sludge — oil residue generated by purifiers, drains and fuel treatment — is Code C. Bilge water — accumulated water in machinery spaces — is Code D (or E if automatic). Transferring sludge and logging it as a bilge operation is a classic finding, and it also makes your quantities stop reconciling.

What a Correct Entry Actually Looks Like

A defensible entry answers four questions without the inspector having to ask: what, how much, from/to where, and when. Compare:

Weak entry (invites questions)Strong entry (closes the question)
"Sludge transferred." Code C — Transfer of oil residues from Sludge Tank No.1 (retained qty 12.4 m³) to Sludge Tank No.2 (retained qty after transfer 18.9 m³); quantity transferred 6.5 m³; date/time; officer signature.
"Bilges pumped out." Code D — Bilge water from Machinery Space Bilge Holding Tank discharged through 15 ppm equipment; quantity 3.2 m³; start/stop position and time; equipment operated normally; officer signature.
"Bunkers received." Code H — Bunkering at [port]; VLSFO 480.0 MT to Tanks 3P/3S; sounding before and after; BDN reference; date/time; officer signature.

Note what the strong entries share: identified tanks, quantities before and after, and a retained quantity that a surveyor can verify against a sounding. That is the difference between a record and an assertion.

Formats vary by flag and by the printed book in use — always follow the instructions printed in your own ORB and any flag-State guidance. The examples above illustrate the level of detail expected, not a mandated wording.

The Ten Mistakes That Generate Deficiencies

  1. Wrong code letter. Above all, sludge logged as bilge (C ↔ D). Fix the understanding, not just the entry.
  2. Missing quantities. An operation without a quantity, and without the tank's retained quantity, cannot be verified.
  3. No weekly residue collection entry. Residues accumulate continuously; a book showing no routine collection for weeks contradicts the machinery's operation.
  4. Retrospective completion. A month of entries in identical ink and handwriting is immediately obvious and is treated as evidence the record is not contemporaneous.
  5. Missing signatures. The officer in charge per entry; the Master per completed page.
  6. Quantities that don't reconcile with sounding books, tank capacity or the previous entry's retained figure.
  7. Unrecorded 15 ppm equipment failures. Code F exists precisely for this; silence looks like concealment.
  8. Improper corrections. Errors should be struck through with a single line, corrected, initialled and dated — never erased, overwritten or covered.
  9. Tank identities that don't match the ship. Names in the book must match the approved plans and the tank markings.
  10. Blank pages or skipped lines that invite the question of what was removed.

What the Inspector Cross-Checks — the Part Nobody Explains

Here is what most guidance omits: the inspector does not read the ORB in isolation. They read it against other records, looking for stories that do not agree. Expect these comparisons:

ORB entryChecked againstWhat a mismatch suggests
Sludge retained quantities (Code C)Sounding book; physical sounding of the tank taken during the inspectionUndeclared disposal, or a book kept for show
Bilge operations (Code D/E)Engine room log; 15 ppm equipment counter/alarm printout; OWS running hoursOverboard discharge not recorded
Bunkering (Code H)Bunker Delivery Notes; fuel consumption figures; noon reportsUnaccounted fuel — a favourite thread to pull
Sludge landed ashoreWaste reception receipts from the port facilityMissing receipts against declared landings
Incinerator useIncinerator log/running hoursDeclared incineration the equipment could not have performed
Equipment defects (Code F)PMS defect and maintenance historyKnown failures absent from one system or the other

This is why ORB integrity is not really a paperwork problem — it is a data consistency problem across several systems. When soundings, maintenance history and bunkering records live in disconnected books and spreadsheets, mismatches are almost guaranteed. Going Electronic: the e-ORB Route

Since IMO Resolution MEPC.312(74), an approved electronic record book may be used in lieu of the hard-copy book. Done properly this removes several whole categories of deficiency at once:

  • Code selection is guided rather than remembered, so C-versus-D errors largely disappear.
  • Mandatory fields prevent entries without quantities or tank identities.
  • Timestamps are tamper-evident, so retrospective completion is structurally impossible.
  • Running balances are calculated, so retained quantities cannot drift out of reconciliation.

Two conditions matter: the system must meet the required standards (audit trail, tamper-proof timestamps, secure authentication, backup), and your flag Administration must issue a Declaration of MARPOL Electronic Record Book which is carried on board. Building ORB Discipline That Survives Crew Changes

  1. Make entries at the operation, not at the desk. The habit is the control.
  2. Sound before and after. Every quantity you write should be one you measured.
  3. Run a monthly self-audit comparing ORB against soundings, engine log and BDNs — exactly the comparisons in the table above. Find your own mismatches first.
  4. Brief every joining engineer on the C/D distinction and this ship's tank naming. Rotation is when discipline slips.
  5. Never leave the book to accumulate. If entries are behind, that is the finding, regardless of what the operations were.
  6. Treat Code F honestly. A recorded, repaired failure is a functioning safety system; an unrecorded one is a concealed defect.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Oil Record Book?

The statutory MARPOL Annex I record of oil-related operations. Part I covers machinery-space operations (ships 400 GT+); Part II covers cargo/ballast operations on oil tankers (150 GT+).

What is the difference between Code C and Code D?

Code C is oil residues (sludge) — collection, internal transfer and disposal. Code D is bilge water accumulated in machinery spaces discharged or transferred non-automatically. Confusing the two is the most commonly cited ORB error.

What are the most common ORB mistakes?

Wrong code, missing quantities and tank identities, no weekly residue collection entry, retrospective completion, missing signatures, and figures that don't reconcile with soundings, engine logs and bunker delivery notes.

Can the ORB be kept electronically?

Yes — following IMO Resolution MEPC.312(74), with a compliant system and a flag-issued Declaration of MARPOL Electronic Record Book carried on board.

How long must it be retained?

MARPOL Annex I requires retention for three years after the last entry, readily available for inspection. Verify current requirements and any flag-specific expectations.
